# De Novo Design and the Two Challenges

**Why this note exists:** "design an antibody" can mean two very different jobs with very
different difficulty. The project is split along exactly that line, and understanding the
split explains most of the plan.

Background: [the target](PD-1%20and%20Checkpoint%20Blockade.md), [the molecule](Antibody%20Architecture.md).

---

## 1. Two meanings of "design"

### Fixed-backbone redesign

A protein's **backbone** is the chain of atoms running through it — the path the chain takes
through space, ignoring the side chains that stick off it. Each position along the backbone
carries one of twenty amino acid **side chains**, and the sequence of those side chains is
what we usually mean by "the sequence".

**Fixed-backbone redesign** means: keep the path exactly as it is, change which amino acids
sit along it. You are re-upholstering the furniture, not moving it.

This is much easier than it sounds like it should be, because the hard part of protein
design — deciding what three-dimensional shape to make — is already solved for you. It was
solved by whoever crystallised the original structure.

### De novo design

**De novo** is Latin for "from new". Here it means designing *both* the backbone and the
sequence, with no template. You decide what shape the molecule should take, and then what
sequence would fold into that shape and bind your target.

This is dramatically harder. You are no longer editing a known good answer; you are proposing
one.

## 2. The two challenges

| | **Challenge 1: "Remix Keytruda"** | **Challenge 2: "Invent the Future"** |
|---|---|---|
| Start from | The solved structure of pembrolizumab bound to PD-1 (PDB 5GGS) | Nothing |
| Task | Redesign the three heavy-chain CDR loops; keep it binding | Design a complete VH/VL antibody against PD-1 |
| Kind of problem | Fixed-backbone redesign | De novo design |
| Safety net | **DockQ** — a score comparing your pose to the real one | **None** |
| Difficulty (organisers') | ★★★☆☆ | ★★★★★ |

## 3. Why Challenge 1 is genuinely easier — and where it is still hard

Challenge 1 hands you the answer to the hardest question. You know:

- what shape the antibody should be
- how it should be oriented against PD-1
- which patch of PD-1 to touch

All you have to do is change the loop sequences without breaking any of that.

**But there is a real tension built into the scoring.** Two of the metrics pull in opposite
directions:

- **Novelty** wants CDR-H3 rewritten as much as possible (top marks below 70% identity)
- **DockQ** wants the binding pose unchanged (top marks at 0.80 and above)

And CDR-H3 supplies 30–50% of the contact surface. So you are being asked to rewrite the most
important part of the interface while leaving the interface intact. *That tension is
Challenge 1.* A method that trivially satisfies one will fail the other.

The useful move is to compute the trade rather than guess it. Because the binding category is
an average over six metrics while novelty is a single metric worth the same weight, one point
of novelty is worth roughly **twice** one point of any single binding metric. Trading a little
pose fidelity for a lot of novelty is usually correct — but you only know that by doing the
arithmetic, which is why we wrote the scoring rules down as data rather than intuition. See
[the metrics note](The%20Eight%20Metrics.md).

## 4. Why Challenge 2 is the one that might not work

No template means no DockQ — there is nothing to compare against, because the right answer
doesn't exist yet. Everything rests on the **confidence** metrics: does the folding model
believe its own prediction of this interface?

Two reasons that is precarious:

**Antibody–antigen prediction is the documented weak spot of folding models.** They are
impressive on proteins generally and noticeably worse here. The likely reason connects back to
[CDR-H3's genetic origin](Antibody%20Architecture.md): because that loop is generated by random
splicing rather than inherited, there is no evolutionary family of related sequences for the
model to learn from. We return to this in [the prediction note](How%20Structure%20Prediction%20Works.md).

**Confidence is not correctness.** A model can be confidently wrong, and nothing in the
scoring catches that. This is important enough to have its own note:
[Confidence Is Not Truth](Confidence%20Is%20Not%20Truth.md).

So Challenge 2 has a live chance of simply not producing a viable design. We decided in
advance to treat that as an acceptable outcome *provided it is characterised* — which stage
fails, at what rate, with what evidence, and what would fix it. A well-analysed negative
result is respectable and more interesting than a lucky hit. An empty folder is not.

## 5. What we are actually doing with Challenge 2

Two routes, one ambitious and one safe.

**Route A — target-conditioned diffusion.** Tools like RFdiffusion (and its antibody-specific
variant RFantibody) can generate new backbones *conditioned on a target*: you tell them which
residues on PD-1 to aim at, and they propose loops that reach them. This is real de novo
design. The risk is purely practical — the software stack is old and our GPU is new.

**Route B — germline framework grafting.** Take a human **germline** framework (the
un-mutated antibody scaffold you are born with, before your immune system starts editing it),
position it using pembrolizumab's known approach angle, and design all six CDRs from scratch
against the target.

Route B is honestly de novo *at the level that matters* — every contact-making sequence is
designed, not copied — while using a framework that real therapeutic antibodies also use. We
will say exactly that rather than implying more novelty than we delivered.

A useful property of Route B: it inherits the right epitope for free. Because it borrows
pembrolizumab's geometry, it automatically aims at the PD-L1-blocking face of PD-1 rather than
discovering at the end that it bound the wrong side.

## 6. One thing we are deliberately not doing

Challenge 1 is fixed-backbone redesign of a structure we have. So you could submit the
**crystal coordinates** with a new sequence label attached and score a near-perfect DockQ by
construction.

We are not doing that, for two reasons. The confidence matrix we submit alongside would not
correspond to those coordinates, making the submission internally inconsistent. And more
importantly, those coordinates are a *measurement of someone else's molecule*, not a
prediction of ours. Recorded here so the temptation is documented rather than rediscovered at
two in the morning.
